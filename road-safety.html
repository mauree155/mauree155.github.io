Case study, public health statistics

Where serious road harm concentrates in Wales

Wales made 20 mph the default on most residential roads in 2023, and the debate since has focused on towns. We asked a different question: across every police reported collision in 2025, where does death and serious injury happen now?

  • 3,004 collisions
  • 4,078 casualties
  • 22 councils

My role

This was a five person group project for the MS4S08 Applied Statistics module at the University of South Wales, analysed in SAS OnDemand for Academics.

I led the visualisation and the poster, and finalised the statistical testing: checking each test's assumptions, confirming the right test for each question, and choosing the chart that shows each result honestly. All eight charts follow one rule: red marks the highest risk group and everything else stays blue, so the eye goes straight to the finding.

The question

In Wales in 2025, are collisions on faster roads more likely to kill or seriously injure someone, and who is most affected?

We measured harm as the KSI rate: the share of collisions in which someone was killed or seriously injured. "Fast roads" means 60 or 70 mph limits, and "slower roads" means 20 to 50 mph. The data is STATS19, the Department for Transport's record of every injury collision reported to the police.

What we found

Across all of Wales, 35.4% of collisions killed or seriously injured someone. On 60 mph roads that rose to 46.2%, the highest of any speed limit. Most of these are rural single carriageways.

A pattern across Wales could just reflect differences between councils, so we tested it two ways. First across councils, then within each council, comparing its fast roads with its own slower roads.

Motorcyclists stood out more than any other road user. They were 12.6% of casualties, but 57.2% of injured motorcyclists were killed or seriously injured.

Choosing the right test

Every test used a 0.05 significance level, with assumptions checked first and a second method run afterwards to make sure the answer did not depend on one choice.

Pearson correlation across councils

r = 0.59, p = 0.004

Both variables passed the Shapiro-Wilk normality check (p > 0.5), so Pearson was appropriate. We used the Department for Transport's adjusted severity, which corrects for police forces recording injuries differently, so councils could be compared fairly. Using recorded severity instead gave r = 0.69.

Paired t test within councils

+6.3 points (95% CI 2.7 to 10.0), t(21) = 3.59, p = 0.002

A paired test compares each council with itself, which cancels out local differences. The differences were normal (Shapiro-Wilk p = 0.11), and a Wilcoxon signed rank test, which makes no normality assumption, gave the same answer (p = 0.002).

Mann-Whitney U on casualty age

Median 38 vs 35 years, p < 0.001

Age was not normally distributed (Shapiro-Wilk p < 0.001), so we used a non parametric test. People killed or seriously injured were slightly older, by about three years (95% CI 1 to 4). With over 4,000 casualties even a small gap is significant, so we reported it as a small effect rather than overstating it.

What it means

Since the 20 mph default, casualties on Welsh 20 and 30 mph roads have fallen. Our results suggest the serious harm that remains sits on rural 60 mph roads, so alongside 20 mph in towns, road safety effort could also target rural 60 mph routes, especially for motorcyclists.

Limitations

  • One year of police reported data only.
  • Fast roads and rural areas overlap almost completely (r = 0.94), so their effects cannot be separated.
  • Casualties in the same collision are not independent of each other.

What I would do next

Extend the analysis over several years and adjust for rurality directly, so speed and rural roads can be separated. I would also build an interactive Power BI dashboard so councils can explore their own figures.
